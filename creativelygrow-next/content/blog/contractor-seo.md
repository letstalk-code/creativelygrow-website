Most SEO advice was written for e-commerce and quietly reused for trades. It talks about traffic, domain authority, and keyword density — none of which are how a homeowner finds someone to coat their garage floor. **Contractor SEO comes down to three things: showing up in the map pack, having a page that helps someone decide, and proving the work is real. Almost everything else sold under the SEO label is a distraction from those three.**

## The map pack is most of the game

Search almost any trade plus a city and look at what's above the fold: three map results. Below that, directories. Your website, if it ranks at all, is usually below the fold.

That means for a local contractor, your Google Business Profile isn't supporting your SEO — it largely *is* your SEO. Ranking there comes down to three inputs:

**Relevance.** Does your profile clearly say you do this work? Primary category is the heaviest lever. An epoxy company categorized as "Flooring contractor" versus "Concrete contractor" will surface for meaningfully different searches. Fill in secondary categories and the services list — they're free and most contractors leave them blank.

**Distance.** Proximity from the searcher to your verified pin. If you're a service-area business with a hidden address, Google still ranks you on that pin. You can't buy your way out of this one, which is why a contractor two towns over outranks you in their town no matter how good your site is.

**Prominence.** Review count and recency, plus how consistently your business shows up across the web. This is the input you have the most control over and it's usually the weakest.

If you do nothing else: get the primary category right, fill out services, and start asking every customer for a review.

## Reviews are ranking, not just reputation

Contractors tend to treat reviews as a reputation thing — nice to have, embarrassing if bad. In local search they're closer to a ranking input.

Two things matter beyond the star average. **Count**, because it's comparative — if the three businesses in your pack have 60, 75, and 90 reviews and you have 8, you are not entering that pack this quarter. And **recency**, because a profile whose newest review is two years old reads as a business that might not exist anymore.

The fix is unglamorous: ask every single customer, at the moment they're happiest, which is usually right after they see the finished work. Not a week later by email. The crew is packing up, the floor looks incredible, the homeowner is standing there — that's the moment.

## Service pages beat a homepage

A lot of contractor sites are one long homepage with everything on it. That page can only rank for one thing, and it's usually the company name.

Build one page per service you actually want to sell. An epoxy contractor might have garage floor coatings, commercial floors, and patio or pool deck coatings. A screen enclosure company might have new pool cage builds, rescreens, and storm damage repair.

One page per service you sell — not per keyword you found. Three real pages beat fifteen thin ones, and thin near-duplicates can pull the whole site down.

Each page should answer what the person is actually asking:

- What does this cost, at least as a range
- How long does it take, and how disrupted is my house
- What does the finished work look like
- What's the process, step by step
- Why should I believe you

That last one is where most contractor sites fail. "Quality craftsmanship since 2009" is not proof. Photos of twelve finished jobs are proof.

## City pages: useful, and easy to get wrong

The instinct is to build a page for every town you serve. Sometimes that works. Usually it produces forty near-identical pages with the city name swapped, which is one of the fastest ways to get filtered out of results entirely.

A city page earns its place when it has something genuinely local: jobs you've actually done there, photos from those jobs, and specifics that only apply to that area. In Florida that might be how pool cage screen holds up to salt air near the coast, or which HOAs in a community have color restrictions.

If you can't write a genuinely different page for a town, don't. Cover it in your service area and move on.

## Where video does real work

Contractors sell something that doesn't exist yet. The customer is buying a promise about how their garage or lanai will look in three weeks, from someone they met once.

Video closes that gap in a way photos can't. A short walkthrough of a finished epoxy floor — the grinding and prep, the flake going down, the finished surface with light moving across it — shows both that the work is real and that the crew knows what they're doing. When we build demo pages for these trades, that's the piece that changes how long people stay on the page.

Two practical notes. Video on your site doesn't directly rank you; it keeps people there and helps them decide, which is worth more. And video posted to your Google Business Profile is a genuine signal of an active business, which most competitors aren't bothering with.

## What to skip

Things sold as contractor SEO that rarely pay for themselves:

- **Buying backlinks.** Directory link packages don't move local rankings and can create problems.
- **Blogging before the foundation exists.** Content on top of a broken profile and no service pages doesn't compound.
- **Chasing national keywords.** Ranking for "epoxy flooring" against manufacturers and national chains isn't a fight worth having. "Epoxy flooring [your county]" is.
- **Redesigns as an SEO strategy.** A new look doesn't fix relevance, distance, or prominence.

## The order that works

1. **Google Business Profile.** Categories, services, service area, real photos, accurate hours.
2. **Reviews.** Start asking today. This is the slowest input to build, so start it first.
3. **Service pages.** One per service, with real prices, real timelines, real photos.
4. **Proof.** Finished-work photos and video on both the site and the profile.
5. **Then content and links**, once there's a foundation for them to compound on.

Most contractors are sold step 5 first, because it's the part that carries a monthly retainer. Starting at step 1 costs nothing and moves more.
