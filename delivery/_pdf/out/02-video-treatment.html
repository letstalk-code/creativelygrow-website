The reality this is built around

One visit. Sixty to ninety minutes on site. No stakeout, no return trip.

A job can run all day and you are not there for all of it. The film is cut from whatever's actually happening in the window you're on site — not from a controlled before-and-after arc that requires planning the visit around the job's schedule instead of yours.

A brand story film is proof, not advertising. The customer is buying a promise about work that doesn't exist yet, from someone they met once. So: real crew, real work, real voice, no stock. That part doesn't change. What changes is how much of the job you need to witness to prove it — the answer is some of it, well shot, not all of it.

Part 2 — Structure

Default 20–30 seconds. Four beats. This is the primary deliverable — native ad length, not a cutdown of something longer.

BeatSecWhat happens
1. Hook0–3Crew mid-task, the most visually interesting thing happening when you arrive. Pattern interrupt, not a logo.
2. Owner + offer3–12Owner to camera: name, business, the offer stated plainly and correctly. This is the spine of the ad.
3. Problem solved12–22Fast-cut b-roll of the crew working — proof the offer is backed by real people doing real work.
4. Close22–30Owner or text card: how to claim it. Phone, site, or both.

The offer line needs to be exact. Everything else on this page is "ask questions, cut the answers" — the offer is the one exception. Confirm the wording with the owner before you roll, get it clean in one or two takes, and don't paraphrase it in the edit.

If the visit produces enough good footage, a longer cut is a bonus, not the goal — see Part 6. Don't plan the shoot around getting one; build it in the edit only if the material supports it.

Part 3 — Shot list & B-roll

Works for any trade. Shoot what's happening when you're there. Nothing below requires arriving before the job starts or staying until it ends.

● = the money shot. If the visit is short, get these first. Vert = shoot 9:16 natively on the day. Reframed 16:9 always looks reframed.

First: find this client's money shot

Every trade has one moment that's genuinely satisfying to watch and that competitors never film. Find it before the shoot with two questions to the owner:

  1. "What part of the job would someone stop and watch?"
  2. "What do you do that a bad crew skips?"

The answer to #1 is your hook. The answer to #2 is what the crew footage should prove.

If they can't answer, look for the moment where the material changes state — liquid to solid, loose to taut, rough to smooth, broken to whole. That's almost always it, and it doesn't require being there for the whole state change — a few seconds of it happening is enough.

The list — get these in the window you have

A. Owner on camera ● — the priority

  • ● The offer, clean, one or two takes, confirmed wording
  • Name, business, one line on what they do
  • One organic credibility line from Part 4, if there's time

B. Crew working ● — most of your footage

  • ●Vert The money shot, 4–5 takes, multiple angles
  • Hands working — no face, just competence
  • The tool doing its job, tight
  • Material texture, macro
  • ● Two people on one task — crews read as capable, soloists read as small
  • Vert Wide of the whole operation
  • Truck, tools coming off the rack, arrival if you're there for it

C. Human ●

  • ● One genuine unguarded moment — someone laughing. Beats ten posed ones
  • Crew talking something through, pointing at the work
  • Logo on truck or shirt, shallow depth

D. Finished work — opportunistic only, never planned around

  • If a finished job is sitting there already (yesterday's, not today's), shoot it
  • If your visit happens to land on a wrap, get it — but don't schedule around this
  • Detail: the edge, the joint, the seam — wherever quality shows, on whatever's finished nearby

A locked-off before/after pair is a bonus if the timing genuinely lines up — you're there right as they start, or right as they finish a short task. It is never worth waiting around for, and it is never required for the ad to work. The owner's offer and real crew footage carry the proof now.

When the job has no visible action to film — service calls, inspections, consultations — lean harder on the owner. The offer line and one organic credibility answer carry the whole piece; b-roll becomes hands, tools, truck, and the customer conversation if one's happening.

Part 4 — Interview questions

For the credibility line only — never the offer, which is confirmed wording, not an ask-and-cut answer.

Ask these. Let them ramble. The usable line is almost always the third sentence of an answer, after they stop performing. You need one good line, not all eight answers.

  1. Tell me what you do, like you're telling a neighbor.
  2. What's the part of the job most people don't see?
  3. What do customers worry about before they call you?
  4. What do you do differently from the guy down the road?
  5. What goes wrong on a bad job — and what do you do about it?
  6. Why should someone pick you?

Q5 is the most valuable question on this list. Everyone answers 4 and 6 with a slogan. Nobody rehearses 5, so that's where the credible answer lives.

Never feed them lines — except the offer. If they say something great, ask them to say it again, don't say it for them. The offer is the one line you do help them land correctly.

Say this out loud when you deliver: the stills matter as much as the film. GBP photos are a ranking and conversion input, and most competitors have none.

Rules

  1. Never script the owner — except the offer. Confirm its wording, don't improvise it on camera.
  2. One visit, 60–90 minutes. No stakeouts, no scheduled return trips for an "after."
  3. Shoot vertical on the day. Reframed 16:9 always looks reframed.
  4. No stock footage. Ever. The entire point is that this is real.
  5. The offer spot is the deliverable. A longer brand story is a bonus if the footage earns it,

never the goal you're shooting toward.
