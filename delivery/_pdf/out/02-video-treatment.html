The rule that governs everything here

A brand story film is proof, not advertising. The customer is buying a promise about work that doesn't exist yet, from someone they met once. The film's only job is to make that promise credible.

So: real crew, real work, real voice. No stock. No "we're passionate about quality." If a line could appear on any competitor's site, cut it.

Part 3 — Shot list & B-roll

Works for any trade. The structure below doesn't change; what fills it does.

● = the money shot. If the day falls apart, get these. Vert = shoot 9:16 natively on the day. Reframed 16:9 always looks reframed.

First: find this client's money shot

Every trade has one moment that is genuinely satisfying to watch and that competitors never film. Find it before the shoot by asking the owner two questions:

  1. "What part of the job would someone stop and watch?"
  2. "What do you do that a bad crew skips?"

The answer to #1 is your hook. The answer to #2 is your proof. Everything else is coverage.

If they can't answer, look for the moment where the material changes state — liquid to solid, loose to taut, rough to smooth, broken to whole. That's almost always it.

The universal list

A. Before ●

  • ● The starting state — locked-off tripod, tape the legs, note lens and height.

You will need this exact frame again and cannot recreate it later.

  • Detail of the problem: the wear, damage, dated finish, whatever they're being paid to fix
  • Wide establishing of the property or space

B. Arrival and setup

  • ● Truck arriving, door opening, boots down
  • Tools coming off the rack
  • Protective covering going down — a trust shot; almost nobody films it and customers notice it
  • Measuring, marking, checking

C. The process ● — most of your footage

  • ●Vert The money shot, 4–5 takes, multiple angles
  • ● The state change itself, close and slow
  • Hands working — no face, just competence
  • The tool doing its job, tight
  • Material texture, macro
  • ● Two people on one task — crews read as capable, soloists read as small
  • Vert Wide of the whole operation

D. After ●

  • ● The exact locked-off before frame, finished. Same lens, height, position
  • ● Light raking across the finished work
  • Detail: the edge, the joint, the seam — where quality actually shows
  • The tactile proof — a hand testing it, pressing it, running along it
  • ● Clean-up: vacuum, broom, wiping down. "They left it spotless" is what customers repeat
  • Vert Slow push or walk through the finished result

E. Human ●

  • Owner in their space, working
  • Crew talking something through, pointing at the work
  • ● One genuine unguarded moment — someone laughing. Beats ten posed ones
  • ● Owner looking at the finished work — the face, not the words
  • Logo on truck or shirt, shallow depth

When the job has no visible transformation — service calls, inspections, consultations — the film leans on people instead: the interview carries it, and b-roll becomes hands, tools, truck, and the customer conversation. Shoot more interview, not more coverage.

Marking the before frame

Tape the tripod legs. Note lens, height, position. A matched before/after from the identical position is worth more than any other pair of shots on the job, and it is the one thing you physically cannot recreate once the work is done.

Part 4 — Interview questions

Ask these. Let them ramble. The usable line is almost always the third sentence of an answer, after they stop performing.

  1. Tell me what you do, like you're telling a neighbor.
  2. How'd you get into this?
  3. What's the part of the job most people don't see?
  4. What do customers worry about before they call you?
  5. What do you do differently from the guy down the road?
  6. Tell me about a job you're proud of.
  7. What goes wrong on a bad job — and what do you do about it?
  8. Why should someone pick you?

Q7 is the most valuable question on this list. Everyone answers 5 and 8 with a slogan. Nobody rehearses 7, so that's where the credible answer lives.

Never feed them lines. If they say something great, ask them to say it again — don't say it for them.

Say this out loud when you deliver: the stills matter as much as the film. GBP photos are a ranking and conversion input, and most competitors have none.

Rules

  1. Never script the owner. Ask questions, cut the answers.
  2. Over-shoot b-roll. You'll never wish you had less. Every ad for the next year comes from this day.
  3. Shoot vertical on the day. Reframed 16:9 always looks reframed.
  4. No stock footage. Ever. The entire point is that this is real.
  5. If the site is a mess, reschedule or clean it. You cannot fix it in the edit, and the client

will blame the film.
